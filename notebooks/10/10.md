# 10. Modeling Scripts

AMPL provides a variety of commands like `model`, `solve`, and `display` that tell the AMPL modeling system what to do with models and data. Although these commands may use AMPL expressions, they are not part of the modeling language itself. In a traditional AMPL session (when AMPL IDE or no APIs were used) they are typed at a prompt, in an environment where you give a command, wait for the system to display a response, then decide what command to give next. With **amplpy**, the Python interface to AMPL, the same operations are available as methods of a Python `AMPL` object, and they are given from Python code: in a script, or in the cells of a notebook such as this one.

You will probably find that your most intensive use of these commands occurs during the initial development of a model, when the results are unfamiliar and changes are frequent. When the formulation eventually settles down, you may find yourself giving the same series of commands over and over to solve and analyze for different collections of data. To accelerate this process, you can capture often-used sequences of commands in a program, and have them repeated automatically, determining how to proceed and when to stop on the basis of intermediate results.

A script is a sequence of commands, captured in a file, to be used and re-used. In this book, scripts are Python programs. Rather than a separate command language, we use Python's own constructs (`for` loops, `if` statements, functions, and string operations) to perform amplpy operations repeatedly and conditionally. Data is passed to AMPL, and results are collected from it, through the Python data structures described in [Chapter 9](../09/09.md): lists, dictionaries, and Pandas DataFrames.

This chapter begins by describing the general principles of working with an AMPL session from Python, including the use of options. [Section 10.1](10_1_general_principles_of_commands_and_options.ipynb) presents these principles, and [Section 10.2](10_2_setting_up_and_solving_models_and_data.ipynb) then presents the operations that you are likely to use most for setting up and solving optimization problems. It also shows how to check the outcome of every solve, a step that becomes essential once solves are performed automatically within a script.

After solving a problem and looking at the results, the next step is often to make a change and solve again. The following two sections explain the variety of changes that can be made without restarting the AMPL session from the beginning. [Section 10.3](10_3_modifying_data.ipynb) describes how to reset data and modify specific data values. [Section 10.4](10_4_modifying_models.ipynb) describes facilities for completely deleting or redefining model components, and for temporarily dropping constraints, fixing variables, or relaxing integrality of variables. (Convenient commands for examining model information can be found in [Chapter 12](../12/12.md), especially in [Section 12.6](../12/12_6_other_display_features_for_models_and_instances.ipynb).)

The remainder of the chapter introduces scripts, using formatted output and sensitivity analysis as examples. [Section 10.5](10_5_running_scripts.ipynb) shows how to collect a sequence of commands into a Python function that can be run again with a single call. [Section 10.6](10_6_iterating_over_a_set_the_for_statement.ipynb) uses `for` loops to repeat commands once for each member of a set, and [Section 10.7](10_7_conditions_and_loop_control.ipynb) shows how to iterate subject to a condition with `while`, how to perform commands conditionally with `if`, and how to terminate a loop early with `break` and `continue`.

Scripts are able to work directly with the sets of character strings that are central to the definition of models. A `for` loop can specify commands to be executed once for each member of some set, for example. To support work with strings, AMPL provides a variety of string functions and operators, which can be used within model declarations as well as in expressions evaluated from a script. Their use is described in the last section of this chapter, [Section 10.8](10_8_manipulating_character_strings.ipynb), together with the corresponding Python string operations.

Most examples in this chapter are based on two models introduced earlier: the diet model of [Chapter 2](../02/02.md) and the multiperiod production model `steelT` of [Section 4.2](../04/4_2_a_multiperiod_production_model.ipynb). Both are by now familiar, so attention can stay on the commands and scripts rather than on the models themselves.
